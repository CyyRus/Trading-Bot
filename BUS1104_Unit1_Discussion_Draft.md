# GDP and Economic Growth: India

India is one of the fastest-growing large economies in the world. According to the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI, 2025), India's real GDP grew 9.2% in FY2023-24 and 6.5% in FY2024-25. Using the Rule of 70 from Chapter 7, a 6.5% growth rate means India's real GDP doubles about every 11 years (70 ÷ 6.5 ≈ 10.8). Below, I look at the factors driving this growth and what it has meant for ordinary people.

## 1. Key factors affecting India's GDP

Using the expenditure approach from Chapter 6, GDP = C + I + G + (X − M). Two components stand out in India's data.

**Private consumption (C)** is the largest component, at roughly 56% of GDP. Private final consumption expenditure grew 7.2% in FY2024-25, up from 5.6% the year before (MoSPI, 2025). With a population of more than 1.4 billion and a growing middle class, household spending is the main engine of demand.

**Investment (I)** makes up roughly a third of GDP, which is high by international standards. Gross fixed capital formation grew 7.1% in FY2024-25 and 9.4% in the fourth quarter alone (MoSPI, 2025). Much of this investment was led by the government (G). The 2024-25 Union Budget set aside ₹11.11 lakh crore for capital expenditure, about 3.4% of GDP, and roads and railways received nearly half of it (ThePrint, 2024).

**Net exports (X − M)** are usually negative for India because it imports large amounts of oil and electronics. Even so, services exports reached a record $387.5 billion in FY2024-25, up 13.6% (Mint, 2025). This shows India's strength in IT and business services.

## 2. How these factors drive growth

Chapter 7 explains long-run growth with the aggregate production function: output depends on physical capital, human capital, and technology (OpenStax, 2022). India's experience fits all three.

- **Physical capital:** Public spending on highways, freight corridors, ports, and airports is a clear case of capital deepening. Better roads lower transport costs for businesses, which raises the productivity of every worker who uses them. This explains why construction was one of the strongest sectors in late FY2024-25.
- **Human capital:** India's large base of English-speaking engineers and graduates built its IT services sector. Firms in Bengaluru and Hyderabad sell software and back-office services worldwide. This is why services exports, rather than manufactured goods, are India's main source of foreign earnings.
- **Technology:** The Unified Payments Interface (UPI) is a strong real-world example. UPI processed about 172 billion transactions in 2024, a 46% increase over 2023 (Business Standard, 2025). Street vendors and small shops can now accept digital payments at almost no cost. This lowers transaction costs and pulls informal activity into the formal economy, where it is counted in GDP.

India also illustrates the convergence idea from Section 7.4. Because it starts from a lower income level, it can adopt existing technologies such as mobile phones and digital payments without developing them from scratch, so it can grow faster than high-income countries.

## 3. Effect of GDP growth on residents' well-being

The clearest benefit is the fall in poverty. Using the World Bank's revised $3-a-day line, India's extreme poverty rate fell from 27.1% in 2011-12 to 5.3% in 2022-23 (World Bank, 2025). Tens of millions of families moved from struggling for basic food toward being able to afford schooling, cooking gas, and mobile phones. Inequality in consumption also narrowed slightly, with the Gini index falling from 28.8 to 25.5 over the same period (World Bank, 2025).

Daily life has changed too. A farmer can now receive government subsidies directly into a bank account and pay suppliers through UPI on a basic smartphone, which reduces both corruption and delays. Better highways cut travel times between cities, which helps workers and small businesses reach larger markets.

However, Section 6.5 of our textbook reminds us that GDP is not a full measure of well-being (OpenStax, 2022). India's rapid growth has come with severe air pollution in cities like Delhi, and much of the workforce remains in informal, low-paying jobs that GDP figures do not fully reflect. Growth has also been uneven across states. In my view, India's GDP growth has clearly improved living standards for millions, but well-being will depend on whether future growth creates good jobs and protects the environment, not just on higher output.
