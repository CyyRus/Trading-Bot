# Comparative Economic Analysis for Economic Convergence: Belize and Trinidad and Tobago

## Introduction

Economic convergence is the idea that lower-income economies can grow faster than higher-income ones and gradually close the income gap (OpenStax, 2022, Section 7.4). This report compares Belize, an upper-middle-income economy, with Trinidad and Tobago (T&T), which the World Bank classifies as high-income. Both are small English-speaking members of CARICOM, but their economies are very different. T&T was built on oil and natural gas, while Belize depends mainly on tourism and agriculture.

## 1. Has Belize experienced economic expansion?

Yes, and by a wide margin. Belize's GDP per capita rose from about $4,770 in 2015 to about $7,680 in 2024, an increase of roughly 61%. The path was not smooth. Belize's tourism-dependent economy collapsed in 2020, when GDP per capita fell to about $4,115. This was a clear business-cycle trough of the kind described in Chapter 6. Recovery was then exceptionally strong: real GDP grew 17.9% in 2021, 8.7% in 2022, 4.7% in 2023, and 8.1% in 2024 (IMF, 2025; World Bank, 2025).
Over the same period, T&T's GDP per capita was nearly flat, rising only about 3%. As a result, the income gap narrowed sharply. In 2015 the average Trinidadian produced about 3.8 times as much as the average Belizean; by 2024 the ratio was about 2.4. This is strong evidence of convergence. However, it happened for two reasons. Belize grew quickly, **and** T&T stagnated: its economy contracted in most years from 2015 to 2021 as oil and gas production declined, and the IMF describes its recent growth as the country's first sustained recovery in about a decade (IMF, 2024). Convergence is therefore partly a story of the richer country slowing down, not only of the poorer country catching up.

## 2. Key factors contributing to convergence in Belize

- **Tourism as a growth engine:** Tourism generates over 40% of Belize's GDP. Overnight arrivals reached a record of roughly 550,000 in 2024, about 21% higher than in 2023 (Belize Tourism Board, 2025). Tourist spending counts as exports in the GDP formula C + I + G + (X − M), so the tourism boom directly raised GDP.
- **Investment and physical capital:** Construction of hotels, resorts, and infrastructure, together with growth in business process outsourcing (BPO), were leading sectors (IMF, 2024). In Chapter 7 terms, this is capital deepening: more capital per worker raises output per worker.
- **Debt reform:** Belize's 2021 "Blue Bond" debt-for-nature swap reduced external debt payments by about US$189 million in exchange for marine conservation commitments (IDB, n.d.). A negotiated discount on Petrocaribe debt also helped. Public debt fell from 103.3% of GDP in 2020 to 61.1% in 2024 (IMF, 2025), which freed up public funds and improved investor confidence.
- **Human capital:** Belize's English-speaking workforce, in a time zone close to North America, attracted call centres and BPO firms that train young workers.

**T&T's policies help explain its slower growth.** Its heavy reliance on energy, which accounts for most of its exports, left it exposed when oil and gas prices and production fell after 2014. The government drew on its sovereign wealth fund (the Heritage and Stabilisation Fund), reduced fuel subsidies, and in 2018 closed the state-owned Petrotrin refinery to cut losses. T&T has long had strong human capital, including the GATE programme for free tertiary tuition. Without a growing energy sector, however, this has not been enough to drive fast growth.

## 3. Implications of convergence for both countries

**For Belize,** convergence has brought real improvements in well-being. Unemployment fell from about 14% in 2020 to 2.1% in 2024 and 1.9% in 2025, and multidimensional poverty fell from 22.1% to 19.1% between 2024 and 2025 (SIB, 2025). However, income distribution remains uneven. In 2025, 27.8% of rural residents were poor compared with 6.5% of urban residents, and Toledo District remains the poorest region. Because Belize depends so heavily on tourism, its gains are also vulnerable. The IMF projected growth of only 1.5% for 2025 and warns that hurricanes and limited hotel and flight capacity are major risks (IMF, 2025).

**For Trinidad and Tobago,** stagnation has had costs despite its higher income. Residents still enjoy a higher average standard of living, better infrastructure, and a higher Human Development Index score than Belizeans. However, slow growth has meant fewer new jobs, shortages of foreign exchange for businesses, and pressure on public finances. Social problems have also grown: the country recorded a record number of murders in 2024, and a state of emergency was declared in December 2024. As Section 6.5 of the textbook notes, GDP per capita alone does not capture quality of life.
## Conclusion

The evidence supports convergence between Belize and Trinidad and Tobago. Belize's income per person rose about 61% over the decade, while T&T's barely changed, cutting the gap from about 3.8 times to about 2.4 times. Belize's progress was driven by tourism, investment, and debt reform. Its future convergence depends on diversifying its economy and spreading the benefits of growth to rural communities.
