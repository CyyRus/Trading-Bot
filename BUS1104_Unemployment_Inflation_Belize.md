# Unemployment and Inflation in a Developing Economy: Belize

## Introduction

Belize is a small developing country that relies on tourism, agriculture, and imports, with its currency fixed at BZ$2 = US$1. This report examines its unemployment and inflation from 2015 to 2025 using Chapters 8 and 9 of OpenStax (2022).

## 1. Current Unemployment Rate and Methodology

Belize's unemployment rate was **1.9% in September 2025**, the lowest on record, down from 2.1% a year earlier (Statistical Institute of Belize [SIB], 2025).

The SIB measures unemployment through its **Labour Force Survey (LFS)**, a household survey usually carried out twice a year, in April and September. It covers all persons aged **14 and older**. A person counts as **unemployed** only if they meet three conditions during the survey reference period: they were (1) without work, (2) available to work, and (3) actively seeking work. Since a 2020 revision, people not searching, such as discouraged workers, are counted as outside the labour force (SIB, 2020). As in Chapter 8:

**Unemployment rate = Unemployed ÷ Labour force (employed + unemployed) × 100**

### Trends and influencing factors

The decade falls into three phases:

- **2015–2019: persistently high unemployment (9–11%).** Growth was slow and the labour force expanded faster than jobs. In 2016, for example, unemployment rose to 11.1% even though more than 3,800 jobs were created, because more people entered the labour force (IMF, 2017).
- **2020: a spike to 13.7%.** COVID-19 shut down tourism, which generates over 40% of GDP, and hotel and tour workers lost their jobs.
- **2021–2025: a rapid decline to 1.9%.** Tourism reached record arrivals, construction and outsourcing (BPO) expanded, and real GDP grew 8.1% in 2024 (IMF, 2025).

## 3. Types of Unemployment

**Current type.** With unemployment at about 2%, **cyclical unemployment is essentially zero**: the economy is at or above full employment. What remains is mainly **frictional** (people between jobs) and **structural**: skill and location mismatches, as rural districts such as Toledo have fewer formal jobs and youth face higher unemployment than adults.

**Short-run vs. long-run.** The **2020 spike was cyclical**: it was caused by a sudden collapse in aggregate demand, especially foreign tourist spending, rather than a lasting change in skills or industries. In the **long run, Belize's main challenge is structural unemployment**, along with seasonal unemployment and underemployment. The causes differ:

- **Short-run (cyclical):** external shocks such as pandemics, hurricanes, or recessions in the U.S., Belize's main tourism market.
- **Long-run (structural):** heavy dependence on tourism and seasonal agriculture such as the sugar cane harvest, a limited manufacturing base, a skills gap between rural workers and service-sector jobs, and a large informal sector.

Inflation was near zero from 2015 to 2020 (with brief **deflation** in 2015), then jumped to 6.3% in 2022 as global oil, food, and shipping prices surged. Because Belize imports most of its fuel and much of its food at a fixed exchange rate, world price increases pass directly into local prices. Inflation eased as world prices stabilized.

### How inflation is computed

The SIB calculates inflation as the **year-over-year percentage change in the Consumer Price Index (CPI)**:

**Inflation rate = (CPI this month − CPI same month last year) ÷ CPI same month last year × 100**

For example, the CPI rose from 105.7 in December 2021 to 112.8 in December 2022, an inflation rate of about 6.7% (SIB, 2023).

### Indices used

The **CPI** is Belize's main cost-of-living index. Its **basket of 395 goods and services** is weighted using the **2018 Household Expenditure Survey**. All items are priced quarterly, in February, May, August, and November. **Food and fuel**, the most volatile categories, are priced monthly. As Chapter 9 notes, a fixed basket can overstate inflation because of **substitution bias** and **quality improvements**.

## 5. Consequences and the Developed-Country Comparison

In 2020, job losses cut household incomes, and emergency relief spending helped push public debt above 100% of GDP. In 2022, inflation reduced **real wages**, hurting the poor most because food and fuel take a larger share of their budgets. Since then, low unemployment and falling inflation have helped cut multidimensional poverty from 22.1% to 19.1% (SIB, 2025), though rural poverty (27.8%) remains far above urban poverty (6.5%).

**If Belize were a developed country, these effects would be milder for three reasons:**

1. **Stronger safety nets.** Unemployment insurance cushions job losses, while many Belizean workers are informal or seasonal with little protection.
2. **Independent monetary policy.** The U.S. Federal Reserve can raise interest rates to fight inflation; Belize's fixed exchange rate means it largely *imports* inflation instead.
3. **Diversification.** A developed economy depends less on one industry, so a tourism shock would not cause a 13.7% unemployment spike.

## Conclusion

Belize has moved from high unemployment and near-zero inflation to record-low unemployment and moderating inflation. Its short-run problems are cyclical shocks, while its long-run challenge is structural dependence on tourism and agriculture.
