# Comparative Economic Analysis for Economic Convergence: India and the United States

## Introduction

Economic convergence is the idea that lower-income countries can grow faster than high-income countries and gradually close the income gap (OpenStax, 2022, Section 7.4). This report compares India, a lower-middle-income economy, with the United States, the world's largest high-income economy, over roughly the last decade (2015–2024). The gap between them is very large, which makes them a useful test of whether convergence is happening.

## 1. Has the less developed country experienced economic expansion?

Yes. India's GDP per capita in current U.S. dollars rose about 70% between 2015 and 2024, from roughly $1,584 to $2,695. Measured in constant 2015 dollars, which removes inflation as explained in Chapter 6, it rose from about $1,584 to $2,397. That is real growth of about 51%, or close to 4.7% per year. By the Rule of 70, India's average income doubles about every 15 years at that pace (70 ÷ 4.7). U.S. real GDP per capita grew roughly 1.5–2% per year, so doubling takes 35–45 years.

The evidence therefore points to **relative convergence**. India is growing about two to three times faster per person, and the income ratio narrowed from about 36:1 to about 32:1. However, the **absolute gap** has widened. In 2024 the average American earned about $83,000 more than the average Indian, compared with about $55,000 more in 2015. Because India starts from such a low base, a higher growth rate still adds fewer dollars than slower growth on a much larger U.S. base. At current rates, full convergence would take many decades.

India's other indicators also suggest healthy expansion. Retail inflation fell to 4.6% in FY2024-25, the lowest since FY2018-19, and unemployment fell from 6.0% in 2017-18 to 3.2% in 2023-24. India runs a large goods deficit, mainly from oil and electronics imports, but a services surplus of about $189 billion offsets much of it. This reflects India's strength in IT and business services.

## 2. Key factors behind convergence in India

Chapter 7 explains growth with the aggregate production function: output depends on physical capital, human capital, and technology. It also notes that poorer countries can catch up by adopting technology that already exists (OpenStax, 2022). India's progress fits this framework.

- **Physical capital and infrastructure:** The central government budgeted ₹11.11 lakh crore, about 3.4% of GDP, for capital expenditure in 2024-25, much of it for roads and railways. This capital deepening lowers transport costs and raises worker productivity.
- **Technology adoption ("catch-up" growth):** India built low-cost digital public infrastructure, including the Aadhaar digital ID, Jan Dhan bank accounts, and the UPI payment system. UPI handled about 172 billion transactions in 2024. These tools let India skip stages of financial development that took rich countries decades.
- **Human capital:** A large, young, English-speaking workforce drives India's services exports, which reached a record $387.5 billion in FY2024-25. The National Education Policy (2020) aims to expand higher education and vocational skills.
- **Policy reforms:** The Goods and Services Tax (2017) created a single national market. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (2016) made it easier to resolve failed businesses. A 2019 corporate tax cut and Production-Linked Incentive schemes (2020) were designed to attract manufacturing, such as smartphone assembly.

U.S. policy over the same period focused on maintaining growth at the technological frontier rather than catching up. Examples include the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (2017), large pandemic relief packages (2020–2021), the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (2021), and the CHIPS and Science Act (2022). After inflation peaked in 2022, the Federal Reserve raised interest rates sharply. This lowered inflation to about 2.9% in 2024 without a recession. Because the U.S. already operates at the frontier, its growth depends on new innovation such as AI and semiconductors. This is why its per capita growth rate is slower, which is consistent with the convergence theory.

## 3. Implications of convergence for both countries

**For India,** faster growth has clearly improved well-being. According to the World Bank, extreme poverty (at $3 per day) fell from 27.1% in 2011-12 to 5.3% in 2022-23, and the consumption Gini index fell from 28.8 to 25.5. Hundreds of millions of people gained bank accounts, cooking gas, and digital payments. However, gains are uneven. Much of the workforce remains in low-paid informal or agricultural jobs, and rapid industrial growth has worsened air pollution in cities such as Delhi. As Section 6.5 of the textbook warns, GDP growth does not automatically capture these quality-of-life costs.

**For the United States,** India's rise creates both opportunities and competition. American companies gain a large and growing consumer market and a deep pool of tech talent. Many U.S. firms, including Apple, Microsoft, and Google, have expanded operations or supply chains in India. At the same time, outsourcing of IT and back-office work puts pressure on some U.S. service jobs. The U.S. also faces its own distribution challenges, since high average income coexists with relatively high inequality.

**Business implications:** Convergence makes India an attractive market for consumer goods, fintech, infrastructure, and education services, because rising incomes create millions of new middle-class customers.

## Conclusion

The data show relative convergence. India is growing faster per person and the income ratio is narrowing, but the absolute gap is still growing. Investment in infrastructure, adoption of digital technology, and market reforms are driving this catch-up. Whether India fully converges will depend on creating productive jobs, improving education quality, and managing the social and environmental costs of growth.
